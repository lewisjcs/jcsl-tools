# Brainstorming reference

The Designer's product lens. Modes, frameworks, and the assumption test. A thinking tool, not a template: pull in what moves the conversation.

## Modes

Pick the mode the conversation is in. Shift when it shifts.

- **Problem exploration.** The problem area is known, the problem is not. Ask who has it and what they do about it today. Separate symptoms from causes: keep asking why until something structural appears. Ask what happens if nothing is done, who solved a version of it elsewhere, and whether it is a problem of awareness, ability, or motivation.
- **Solution ideation.** The problem is defined. Generate at least five distinct approaches before judging any. Vary them by scope (small tweak against big bet), by kind (product, process, policy), and by timing (quick win against long investment). Include one that does the opposite and one that removes something.
- **Assumption testing.** A direction exists. List every assumption it rests on, stated and unstated. For each: how confident, what evidence, what would disprove it. Name the riskiest one, the one that kills the idea if wrong, and the cheapest way to test it before building. Argue the strongest case against the idea once.
- **Strategy.** Direction or a big bet. Map the possible moves, not only the obvious one. Think in bets: what is wagered, the odds, the payoff. Ask what a move enables or forecloses, and what the right move is at three months, one year, three years.

## Frameworks

- **How might we.** "How might we <outcome> for <user> without <constraint>?" Too broad means anything, too narrow is a solution in disguise. Write five to ten from one problem; each reframing opens a different space.
- **Jobs to be done.** "When <situation>, I want to <motivation> so I can <outcome>." The job is stable while solutions change. Functional jobs are easy to see; emotional and social ones are often stronger. Ask what was fired to hire this.
- **Opportunity solution tree.** Outcome, then opportunities from evidence, then several solutions per opportunity, then several cheap experiments per solution. One solution per opportunity means the exploring stopped early.
- **First principles.** State the assumption, break it into its parts, ask of each whether it is a law or a convention, rebuild from the parts that survive.
- **Inversion.** Ask how to make the problem worse. Reverse each answer.
- **SCAMPER.** Substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to other use, eliminate, reverse.

## Assumption categories to probe

User ("they want this": how many, what evidence). Problem ("it is real": how often, how much they care). Solution ("this will work": why this, what was dismissed). Value ("it moves the number": which number, by how much, by when). Feasibility ("we can build it": in what time, at what trade). Adoption ("they will find and use it": how, what behavior changes).

## The session rhythm

Frame, diverge, provoke, converge, capture. Framing prevents wasted divergence. Divergence is quantity without judgment. Provoking is the sparring: the strongest counter-argument, who would hate it, what is not being seen, the ten times more ambitious version. Converging groups, weighs against impact, feasibility, alignment, and evidence, and keeps the top two or three with their biggest unknown and its cheapest test. Capturing writes what mattered, the assumptions to test, the questions to research, and what was set aside on purpose.

## Traps to name aloud

Solutioning before framing. Copying a competitor's feature instead of serving the need. Anchoring on a constraint during divergence. The one-idea brainstorm. Analysis without convergence. Brainstorming a question that needs data instead.
